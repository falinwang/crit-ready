# Chapter 5: Get in the Right Mindset

## Core Idea
Before responding to stakeholders, designers must mentally prepare themselves by giving up control, checking their ego at the door, and prioritizing collaboration over defense. A positive mindset allows you to facilitate a discussion about design solutions rather than merely absorbing and reacting to criticism.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Thank, Repeat, Prepare**: A transitional formula used right before providing a response to stakeholder feedback.
  - When to use: At the start of your response, after a stakeholder finishes giving their feedback and before you launch into your defense or explanation.
  - How: First, thank them for their time and insights. Second, repeat/summarize their feedback in a complimentary way to show you were listening. Third, prepare them by foreshadowing the content and direction of your upcoming response (e.g., "I'd like to explain why we did what we did...").

## Key Concepts
- **Facilitator Role**: The understanding that a designer's role in a meeting is not to defend work or absorb changes, but to guide the conversation to a positive place and champion great design.
- **Taxation Without Representation**: When stakeholders discuss and modify designs in other meetings without the designer present or without being properly equipped to explain the design decisions.
- **Ego Management**: Separating yourself from your ideas in a way that allows others to inform the project, avoiding assumptions that stakeholders' ideas are invalid because they aren't designers.
- **The "Yes Reflex"**: The habit of beginning every response with a positive affirmation of the other person's idea or request, reinforcing that everyone is on the same team.
- **Positive Persona**: Being likable, approachable, and projecting a sense of self that appeals to stakeholders (e.g., smiling, being authentic, using humor).
- **Stakeholder Empathy**: Walking around to the other side of the table to view the problem from the stakeholder's perspective and needs.

## Mental Models
- **Improvisational Comedy (The "Yes, and..." approach)**: Conversations with stakeholders are improvisations. You must agree with the premise they bring (leading with a "yes") to keep the conversation going; shutting them down stops the progress.
- **The Egotistical Reaction vs. The Collaborative Reaction**: An egotistical reaction assumes the stakeholder doesn't understand the job and therefore their feedback is invalid. A collaborative reaction recognizes their idea has value and works to find a solution based in logic, not stereotypes.
- **Stakeholders as Design Representatives**: Recognizing that stakeholders will inevitably have to present your work to others (bosses, peers). You must engineer their confidence by equipping them with the right vocabulary and tools to defend the design in your absence.

## Anti-patterns
- **"You're wrong"**: Outright telling stakeholders they are wrong makes them defensive. It creates an adversarial environment.
- **"From a design perspective..."**: Creates a separation between the designer's expertise and the stakeholder, coming across as a superiority claim. It also focuses on the designer's perspective rather than the user's.
- **"Like" and "Don't like"**: Focusing on personal preferences rather than what works or doesn't work for the user experience.
- **"Yes, but..."**: Saying "but" immediately negates the "yes" and is generally perceived as a "no."
- **Too much jargon**: Using industry-specific terms (Agile, modals, CTA) alienates stakeholders. It fails to meet them at their level of understanding.

## Worked Example

**Engineering Confidence Example**: The author was consulting for an engineering director who continually asked, "Where are the designs?" despite having approved user flows and wireframes for a month. The author initially felt defensive, explaining the UX process repeatedly. However, after the director admitted, "Every week when I go to my senior leadership meeting I have nothing to show," the author realized the real problem. The director didn't lack designs; he lacked the confidence to represent user flows to his peers. The author shifted the deliverables to ensure the director always had something he could confidently present as his own work, engineering his confidence and saving the project from being cut.

## Key Takeaways
1. Your job is to lead a discussion on solutions, not cram your work down everyone else's throat or silently absorb criticism.
2. Accept that you do not have ultimate control over the final outcome, which creates an emotional release and helps you communicate objectively.
3. Check your ego at the door; believing that your ideas are the only good ones prevents you from seeing the value in stakeholder suggestions.
4. Always lead with a "yes" to establish that you are on the same team and to buy yourself time to figure out a solution.
5. Never use the word "like" in design discussions; focus entirely on what works or doesn't work for the user.
6. Adopt a positive persona by smiling to convey confidence, being authentic, and using lighthearted humor to reduce tension.
7. Use the "Thank, Repeat, Prepare" formula to gracefully transition into your design defense.
