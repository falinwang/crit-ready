# Chapter 2: Stakeholders Are People Too

## Core Idea
To effectively communicate with stakeholders, you must improve the quality of your relationships with them, earning trust and establishing rapport. By applying the same empathy you reserve for your end-users to the people who hold the keys to your project's success, you can navigate their feedback more productively.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Four Steps to Seeing Their Perspective**: A method for breaking down the adversarial wall between designers and stakeholders to build a foundation of empathy.
  - When to use: Before engaging in deep design reviews or when a stakeholder relationship feels strained or adversarial.
  - How: 
    1. **See them as human**: Recognize they have personal lives, stressors, and limited brain capacity outside of your project.
    2. **Create shared experiences**: Find common ground by spending time outside the office context (e.g., lunch, coffee, casual conversations).
    3. **Develop empathy**: Shift from a defensive posture to one of solidarity, actively wanting to help them succeed in their specific roles.
    4. **Ask good questions**: Ask personal and professional questions (e.g., "What's your priority for this project?") to understand their underlying motivations.

## Key Concepts
- **Team Influencers**: People within your immediate influence (fellow designers, developers, PMs) who you interact with daily and who form your primary support network.
- **Executive Influencers**: People who oversee the project (directors, VPs, CEOs). Their support is critical, but they have limited time and often context-switch heavily.
- **External Influencers**: People outside the core team (accounting, spouses, friends) who influence the project indirectly via proxy opinions.
- **Stakeholder Values**: The specific priorities that drive different roles (e.g., developers value efficiency and lack of rework; product owners value innovation and business goals).

## Mental Models
- **The Attribution Concept**: The psychological tendency to see our own behavior as situational while viewing others' behavior as a reflection of their permanent personality traits. Designers often unfairly dismiss stakeholders as "just not getting design" instead of recognizing their situational constraints.
- **Stakeholder Empathy**: Viewing your role as a designer not as a defender of mockups, but as a partner whose ultimate job is to make the stakeholders successful in their respective goals.

## Anti-patterns
- **Stereotyping by Job Title**: Why it fails: Assuming every executive or developer fits a rigid mold prevents you from uncovering the unique, situational factors driving their feedback on a specific day.
- **Being Too Professional**: Why it fails: Never letting your guard down or failing to share your own outside interests prevents the formation of authentic rapport, making difficult design conversations much harder to navigate.

## Worked Example

The author describes working with an executive who was a visionary thinker but perpetually underwhelmed by the team's simple Minimum Viable Product (MVP). Because the author empathized with the executive's high-level view and desire for a grand endgame, he adjusted his approach. He began bringing two mockups to every meeting: the modest MVP and an innovative, no-limits "preferred future" design. By leading with the grand vision, he kept the executive excited, making it much easier to get support for the immediate, realistic MVP step.

## Key Takeaways
1. A stakeholder's negative reaction to your work often has an underlying situational explanation completely unrelated to your design.
2. Creating shared experiences—even just asking about a hobby or grabbing coffee—dramatically softens future project disagreements.
3. You must tailor your communication approach based on stakeholder roles; focus on timelines for PMs, effort for developers, and business growth for executives.
4. Simple gestures like handwritten thank-you notes or inexpensive, personalized gifts can make a lasting impression and build deep trust.
5. Asking direct questions like "How does this project affect your job?" can uncover hidden agendas (e.g., budget pressures) that explain confusing feedback.
