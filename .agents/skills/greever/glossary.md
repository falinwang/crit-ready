# Glossary

- **A Seat at the Table** — Elevating design leadership to executive tiers (e.g., Chief Design Officer or VP of Design) so user experience and design thinking inform corporate strategy and business decisions directly rather than remaining downstream execution (Ch 11).
- **Anticipated Objections Matrix** — A proactive preparation artifact listing expected stakeholder questions, criticisms, and concerns per screen/feature, paired with planned rationale and responses before entering the meeting room (Ch 3).
- **Appeal to a Nobler Motive** — A response strategy where design decisions are explicitly attached to agreed-upon business goals, KPIs, or core problems rather than personal preference (Ch 6).
- **Appeal to the Business** — The fourth step in the IDEAL framework that explains how the design decision impacts business metrics, conversion, operational costs, efficiency, or organizational KPIs (Ch 8).
- **Articulating Design Decisions** — The practice of intelligently explaining the rationale behind design choices to stakeholders in a way that is compelling, demonstrates expertise and intentionality, fosters agreement, and secures support to move forward (FM, Ch 1).
- **Ask Others to Weigh In** — A tactic of soliciting input directly and neutrally from supportive colleagues or domain experts in the room to build consensus without directly contradicting a stakeholder (Ch 6).
- **Async Video Walkthrough** — A recorded 2.5-to-5-minute screen recording with picture-in-picture webcam narration and a concise script, used instead of static chat/email attachments to preserve tone, nuance, and intent during remote/async reviews (Ch 3).
- **Attribution (Fundamental Attribution Error)** — The psychological concept describing how people understand behavior: individuals naturally attribute others' behavior to personality traits ("they don't get design"), while viewing their own behavior as situational (Ch 2).
- **Bank Account of Trust (Emotional Bank Account)** — Stephen Covey’s interpersonal metaphor applied to stakeholder relationships: positive interactions, agreements, successful metrics, and beautiful designs act as deposits, while disagreements, pushbacks, and missed deadlines act as withdrawals (Ch 10).
- **Benefit/Risk Framing** — Phrasing an agreement question to highlight either the positive benefits of agreeing (e.g., higher conversion) or the negative risks of disagreeing (e.g., project delay, friction) (Ch 8).
- **Bike-Shedding** — A software and design term derived from Parkinson’s Law of Triviality, describing the tendency of meeting attendees to fixate on minor, superficial, and easily understood details because they are visible and accessible to everyone's opinion (Ch 10).
- **Checking Ego at the Door** — Mentally separating self-worth and personal identity from design deliverables, remaining open to good ideas from non-designers, and resisting dismissive justifications (Ch 5).
- **Cognitive Load (Meeting Usability)** — The available mental capacity of stakeholders during a meeting. Just like software UI design, meetings must be designed to strip away clutter, ambiguity, and roadblocks (Ch 3).
- **Common Design Patterns** — Widely recognized UI conventions or design system components that provide consistency, lower cognitive load, and reduce engineering cost (Ch 7).
- **Context Setting** — The structured five-part opening of a design meeting (state goal, summarize last meeting, show process timeline, specify feedback needed, restate goal) designed to quickly orient distracted or context-switching stakeholders before presenting designs (Ch 3).
- **Conversation Facilitator (Design Role)** — The mental model that a designer's role in reviews is not to passively absorb criticism, defend work, or pitch like a salesperson, but to guide a collaborative, positive discussion toward effective design solutions (Ch 5).
- **Converting "Likes" to "Works"** — The conversational technique of redirecting subjective stakeholder preference feedback ("I don't like X") into objective evaluations of design effectiveness and utility ("Why doesn't X work?") (Ch 4).
- **Decisive Default (Default with Deadline)** — Announcing a specific decision and setting an explicit deadline (e.g., "If I don't hear back by 5 PM, I'm moving forward with this") to overcome consensus deadlock (Ch 9).
- **Demonstrate Effectiveness** — A response strategy answering "Why is this better than the alternative?" by visually showing differences and functional advantages through side-by-side mockups or interactive prototypes (Ch 6).
- **Design-by-Committee** — A failure mode where unchecked feedback, groupthink, and competing stakeholder opinions spiral into a compromise-driven hodgepodge that cripples the user experience (Ch 1).
- **Direct Ask ("Do You Agree?")** — A closing communication technique that pauses meeting chatter and forces stakeholders into a decisive response rather than leaving an open-ended debate (Ch 8).
- **Discreet Filtering (Applying Filters)** — The deliberate assessment and intentional omission of irrelevant tangents, out-of-scope brainstorming, or off-the-wall remarks from the official meeting follow-up record (Ch 9).
- **"Do Something, Even If It's Wrong"** — A bias toward decisive action when faced with stakeholder indecision or ambiguity; making a clear choice to force the team out of paralysis (Ch 9).
- **Dress Rehearsal (Practice Out Loud)** — The practice of verbally speaking presentation points and answering mock objections out loud in an empty room, car, or meeting space prior to the actual session (Ch 3).
- **Element of Surprise** — Introducing an unexpected element (well-timed levity, surprising imagery, quiz format, or physical prop) to break conversational monotony and anchor critical takeaways without becoming a distraction (Ch 3).
- **Empathize with the User** — The third step in the IDEAL framework that grounds the decision in the user's mindset, task constraints, environmental context, or cognitive load (Ch 8).
- **Empathy (for Stakeholders)** — Looking at a design project through the stakeholder's reality and needs, shifting the designer's communication posture from defense to solidarity (Ch 2).
- **Empowerment Sweet Spot** — The optimal leadership threshold for delegating decision-making authority to designers, defined as being just past the leader's personal comfort level of giving up control (Ch 11).
- **Executive Influencers** — Higher-level decision-makers (VPs, directors, C-level executives) who oversee the project from a 30,000-foot strategic view, suffer high cognitive switching costs between topics, and hold ultimate decision-making authority (Ch 2).
- **Existing Data** — Analytics and historical metrics already available before design work begins, used to formulate informed hypotheses (Ch 7).
- **Explicit Listening** — The practice of using outward, observable verbal and physical behaviors—such as taking detailed notes, asking clarifying questions, and repeating or rephrasing statements—to demonstrate engagement and ensure mutual understanding (Ch 4).
- **External Influencers** — Parties outside the immediate core project who lack deep project context but influence decisions via proxy or casual feedback (Ch 2).
- **Falling on Your Sword** — The leadership tactic of voluntarily stepping up to accept blame for an ambiguous or team-wide failure in order to halt circular finger-pointing, defuse blame games, and allow the team to move on to solutions (Ch 10).
- **Follow Up Fast** — The practice of distributing a written summary within an hour (or at most 24 hours) of a meeting to record decisions, assign ownership, and prevent retroactive second-guessing (Ch 9).
- **Forcing Their Hand** — Using direct, consequence-focused questions to compel non-committal, hesitant, or passive-aggressive stakeholders to declare a clear stance (Ch 8).
- **Give Them a Choice (Rubik's Cube Principle)** — A tactic that leverages loss aversion by presenting a stakeholder's request alongside the explicit trade-off or sacrifice required (Ch 6).
- **Giving Up Control** — The intentional recognition that designers rarely have the final unilateral say over a product, releasing personal attachment to lower defensiveness and relying instead on communication and influence (Ch 5).
- **Goal-Reason Formula** — The messaging structure `"[design] will affect [goal] because [reason]"`, used to tie UI choices directly to business outcomes (Ch 7).
- **Good Communicators Win** — The organizational reality that the most articulate person usually wins the argument; superior ideas that are poorly explained will consistently lose to inferior ideas presented with compelling rhetoric (Ch 1).
- **Hairy Arms** — A historical animation/design tactic where deliberate, distracting flaws are added to designs the creators do not want approved, leading directors to reject them and approve the clean, preferred designs (Ch 10).
- **Hearing What Isn't Being Said** — The skill of detecting subtext, unstated reservations, organizational politics, or indirect cues masked behind polite phrasing or questions (Ch 4).
- **Home Page Syndrome** — A condition where the primary landing screen becomes a cluttered catchall for every department’s competing priorities and links, degrading overall usability (Ch 1).
- **IDEAL Response** — A five-step structured framework for articulating design decisions and responding to stakeholder feedback: Identify the problem, Describe your solution, Empathize with the user, Appeal to the business, and Lock in agreement (Ch 8).
- **Identify the Problem** — The first step in the IDEAL framework where the designer briefly and clearly restates the specific problem the design solves to ensure everyone is aligned before debating solutions (Ch 8).
- **Implicit Listening** — The practice of actively listening and deriving meaning from stakeholder feedback without outwardly performing specific visible or verbal actions (Ch 4).
- **Including the Why** — The practice of documenting the underlying rationale and business/user justification behind every design decision directly in meeting notes to prevent re-litigating decisions in future meetings (Ch 4).
- **Key Messages** — Common, reusable templates and thematic explanations categorized by Business, Design, Research, and Limitations that justify design decisions during stakeholder discussions (Ch 7).
- **Leading with a YES** — An interaction philosophy derived from leadership and improv comedy where every response begins with positive affirmation ("Yes...") to foster collaboration and affirm common goals (Ch 5).
- **Lock in Agreement** — The final step in the IDEAL framework where the designer directly and unambiguously asks stakeholders for verbal confirmation and buy-in to move forward (Ch 8).
- **Management by Walking Around (MBWA)** — An informal executive leadership style where leaders stay physically and socially close to teams, creating frequent, low-friction interactions that improve design alignment (Ch 11).
- **Managing Expectations** — The practice of proactively setting, adjusting, and communicating progress, thought processes, timelines, and trade-offs so stakeholders always have confidence and visibility (Ch 10).
- **McDonald's Theory** — Jon Bell's concept that proposing an obviously unappealing, mediocre, or provocative default option breaks group politeness and compels people to voice their true preferences (Ch 9).
- **Note Buddy** — A designated colleague or cross-functional partner who attends design meetings to capture notes and decisions, freeing the presenter's cognitive capacity (Ch 4).
- **Painting a Duck** — A deliberate design tactic of inserting an obnoxious, obvious, or extraneous element into a design solely to attract management attention and be removed, thereby protecting the rest of the design (Ch 10).
- **Parkinson’s Law of Triviality** — The principle stating that people in a committee or meeting will spend a disproportionate amount of time on trivial, easy-to-grasp issues while neglecting the complex, central, and less-trivial problems (Ch 10).
- **Placeholder Distraction** — Any non-essential design artifact (Lorem ipsum copy, generic stock photos) that inadvertently captures stakeholder attention and derails strategic discussion (Ch 3).
- **Positive Persona** — Presenting an approachable, authentic, likable, and confident demeanor—using smiles, kindness, lighthearted humor—to earn trust and disarm tension in meetings (Ch 5).
- **Post-Meeting One-on-Ones** — Pulling key stakeholders or unexpected allies aside immediately after a meeting while the context is fresh to build coalitions and clarify unresolved items (Ch 9).
- **Postpone the Decision** — A tactic of buying time (hours or days) or pausing a meeting when a conversation devolves into design-by-committee, allowing the designer to iterate calmly and present better options (Ch 6).
- **Primacy and Recency** — Cognitive memory principles establishing that listeners retain the first and last parts of a presentation best, while retention drops in the middle (Ch 3).
- **Primary vs. Secondary Use Cases** — The practice of optimizing the UI hierarchy for the core, most frequent user tasks while intentionally subordinating secondary or edge cases (Ch 7).
- **Process Timeline (Fidelity Spectrum)** — A visual artifact displayed during context setting to anchor stakeholders on where the project is between early discovery and final polish (Ch 3).
- **Propose an Alternative** — A response tactic where the designer accepts the underlying problem behind stakeholder feedback but presents an elevated, refined design solution rather than implementing the suggestion literally (Ch 6).
- **Reflective Data** — Before-and-after metrics collected post-implementation to verify whether design changes achieved their intended measurable impact (Ch 7).
- **Relational Discernment** — Evaluating feedback based on the speaker's true intentions, organizational influence, team consensus, and likelihood of bringing it up again (Ch 9).
- **Represent the User** — A strategy of acting as the active advocate and spokesperson for users by translating usability insights into tangible human stories that generate stakeholder empathy (Ch 6).
- **Resource Constraints (Support, People, Money, Time)** — The four structural limitations cited to justify scoped-down designs (Ch 7).
- **Ripple Effect** — The interconnected nature of digital product design where altering a single element or interaction triggers cascading rework across surrounding layouts, navigation flows, and technical dependencies (Ch 11).
- **Rule of Three (Repetition)** — The practice of deliberately reinforcing the primary goal and the core design decision at least three times (verbally and visually) throughout a presentation to ensure retention (Ch 3).
- **Scroll-Jacking** — Overriding standard browser scrolling behavior with custom script-based navigation, often cited as a negative anti-pattern violating web standards (Ch 7).
- **Shared Experiences** — The practice of creating common ground with stakeholders outside the normal pressure and context of work to reframe adversaries into allies (Ch 2).
- **Shared Vocabulary** — A mutually understood set of terms established by meeting stakeholders where they are, adopting their comfortable language, and gradually introducing precise UX concepts (Ch 4).
- **Show a Comparison** — A response tactic of placing the proposed design and the stakeholder's suggestion side-by-side on the same canvas with a dividing line to let visual differences speak for themselves (Ch 6).
- **Stakeholder Representation** — The principle that designers must equip their stakeholders (managers, PMs, engineers) with the vocabulary, rationale, and artifacts needed to confidently defend and represent the design decisions to outside executives or other teams (Ch 5).
- **Stakeholder Stories** — An adaptation of agile user stories for stakeholders to systematically analyze what each role cares about and tailor design rationale accordingly (Ch 2).
- **Standards Compliance** — Justifying design choices by citing adherence to technical, accessibility (WCAG), or platform standards (Ch 7).
- **Subjectivity of Design** — The principle that visual taste and aesthetics are inherently subjective; therefore, designs cannot be defended on aesthetics alone, but must be anchored to business goals and user impact (Ch 1).
- **Support (vs. Approval / Consensus)** — Securing stakeholder trust and agreement to move forward with a plan or solution, even if stakeholders disagree on specific aesthetic details (Ch 1).
- **Support vs. Agreement** — The principle that universal agreement on every design detail is impossible; success is securing stakeholder *support* and agreement to move forward with the proposed solution (Ch 8).
- **Team Influencers** — Immediate cross-functional peers (fellow designers, developers, PMs) who serve as a designer's closest allies, sounding boards, and cultural foundation (Ch 2).
- **Testing Highlight Reel** — A curated, edited video clip (seconds to a minute) from usability studies presented to stakeholders to deliver fast, indisputable proof of user behavior (Ch 7).
- **Thank, Repeat, Prepare (TRP)** — A structured, concise three-part transitional framework delivered immediately before giving a design response: thank the stakeholder for their input, repeat/summarize their point complimentarily, and prepare/foreshadow the response structure (Ch 5).
- **The Art of the Pause** — Intentionally remaining silent for two to three seconds after a stakeholder finishes speaking to ensure they have completed their thought, let the conversation settle, and demonstrate thoughtful consideration before answering (Ch 4).
- **The Big Three** — The three foundational criteria required for any design to be successful in an organization: (1) It solves a problem, (2) It is easy for users, and (3) It is supported by everyone (Ch 1).
- **The CEO Button** — An unexpected, top-down executive request to insert an unvetted feature or button that disrupts project balance, undermines usability, and bypasses the design rationale (Ch 1).
- **The First Question ("What are we trying to communicate?")** — The foundational inquiry that must precede any discussion of visual execution or technical constraints, aligning the project on core messaging and business intent (FM).
- **The Four "izes"** — The four foundational responsibilities executives and nondesigners have to help designers succeed: Realize we have expertise, Prioritize our needs, Authorize the entire team to move quickly, and Recognize that we are people, too (Ch 11).
- **The Interface Is Your Interface** — The reality that because UI/UX work is visually exposed, every stakeholder naturally forms opinions on it, making communication about the work as critical as doing the work (Ch 1).
- **The Meeting After the Meeting** — The informal, post-adjournment window (in hallways, after-call debriefs, or desk drop-ins) where attendees share candid feedback, make deals, or offer support outside group hierarchy (Ch 9).
- **The Three Core Questions** — The preparation framework every designer must answer before presenting: (1) What problem does it solve? (2) How does it affect the user? (3) Why is it better than the alternative? (Ch 1).
- **Usability Testing** — Task-based, observational research in which designers watch representative users interact with a product to identify interaction flaws (Ch 11).
- **User Flow Preservation** — The rationale that interface decisions must maintain a smooth, predictable, and linear progression toward task completion without introducing disjointed backtracking (Ch 7).
- **Yes Reflex** — The trained conversational habit of opening with "Yes" rather than a default "No," which buys cognitive time to assess feasibility and validates the other person without falsely committing to an ineffective solution (Ch 5).
